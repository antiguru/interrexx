The interrexx Language Specification

Operators and special characters

ANSI: 6.2.2; rexxref: General Concepts > Structure and General Syntax > Tokens

An operator character is one that can be part of an operator, and a special character is one that stands alone as a token, such as a parenthesis. Both kinds end the token before them, so no blank is needed beside them. Several operators are written with two or three characters, and the characters of one operator may be spread over blanks and comments. This page gives the operators as tokens, the way characters are joined into them, and the way blanks next to these characters are treated. What an operator computes is the business of the numbers and the expressions chapters.

[lex.operators.set]

The operators are these tokens. The arithmetic operators are + - * / % // and **. The concatenation operator is ||. The comparison operators are = \= > < >= <= <> >< \> and \<, and the strict comparison operators == \== >> << >>= <<= \>> and \<<. The logical operators are & | and &&. The characters + - and \ are also prefix operators. The blank and the abuttal of two terms are operators too, but they are not tokens, as the blank rule explains.

say 7 + 2 7 - 2 7 * 2 7 / 2 7 % 2 7 // 2 7 ** 2
say (2>1) (2<1) (2>=1) (2<=1) (2<>1) (2><1) (2\>1) (2\<1) (2=2) (2\=2)
say (2==2) (2\==2) (2>>1) (2<<1) (2>>=1) (2<<=1) (2\>>1) (2\<<1)
say (1&0) (1|0) (1&&0) (\0) (-3) (+3) 'a'||'b'

[lex.operators.longest]

A run of operator characters is split from the left into the longest operators of the set that fit. A run that cannot be split that way, because a character other than < or > is left over with no operand, such as &&&, ====, \>= or <==, raises SYNTAX 35.1. A single < or > that is left over begins a variable reference term instead. That term takes a symbol, and the clause raises SYNTAX 20.930 when none follows. A leftover << or >> is an operator with no left operand and raises SYNTAX 35.1, as 1 =<< 2 does.

x = 5
say (1<=-1) (1==-1) (2>-1) (1=\0) (1=>x)
call try '1 => 2'
call try '1 &&& 0'
call try '1 ==== 1'
call try '1 \>= 0'
call try '1 <== 0'
call try '1 =<< 2'
exit

[lex.operators.gap]

The characters of a two-character or three-character operator may be separated by blanks, tabs and comments. The separators are dropped, and the run of operator characters that remains is split as the longest rule says. So > = is >= and = = is ==, while - - is two prefix minus signs and \ \ two nots, because -- and \\ are no operators, and 1 > = - 1 is >= followed by a prefix minus.

say (2 > = 1) (2 = = 2) (2 \ = 1) (2 > < 1) (2 < /* c */ < 3)
say 2 | | 3 1 |/**/| 2
say (- - 1) (\ \ 0) (+ + 4) (1 > = - 1) (1 \ = \ 1)

[lex.operators.comment]

The two characters /* always open a comment, so an operator ends before them. A / before a comment is the division operator, and //* is not the remainder operator followed by *. The comment is then a separator between characters as the gap rule describes.

say 3 */**/* 2
say 7 //**/ 2
say 7 / /* c */ / 2

[lex.operators.not]

Wherever the backslash starts an operator, as the prefix not or in \=, \==, \<, \>, \<< and \>>, the byte 'AA'x or 'AC'x may stand in its place, as the not rule says.

n = 'aa'x 'ac'x
do i = 1 to 2
  c = word(n, i)
  interpret 'say (' || c || '0) (1' c'= 2) (1' c'== 2)',
            '(1' c'<< 2) (1' c'>> 2)'
end

Special characters

[lex.operators.special]

The special characters are the comma, the semicolon, the colon, the parentheses ( and ), the brackets [ and ], and the tilde. Each is a token that ends the token before it. The tilde is the message operator ~, and two tildes ~~ are the cascade operator. As the characters of any operator may be, the two tildes may be separated by blanks and comments.

say 'abc'~length 'abc'~~length 'abc'~ ~length 'abc'~/**/~length

3 abc abc abc
[lex.operators.blank]

A blank, or a run of blanks and tabs, between two terms is the concatenation operator. A blank next to an operator, a comma or a tilde has no effect. So does a blank after an opening ( or [ and one before a closing ) or ]. A comment is no blank.

say 'a'   'b' 'a'||'b' 'a'/**/'b'
say 1+1 1  +  1
say left( 'abc' , 2 ) 'abc' ~ left( 2 )
a = .array~of(5, 6)
say a[ 2 ]

[lex.operators.adjacent]

A ( that follows a symbol or a string with no blank between them opens the arguments of a function call, and a [ that follows a term with no blank between them opens a subscript. A ( that follows the name in a message term in the same way holds the arguments of the message. A comment between them is no blank. With a blank, a ( starts a parenthesized term that is concatenated with the term before it, so that a message is sent without arguments, and a [ is an error, SYNTAX 35.1. A ( after a ) with no blank between them concatenates the two terms without a blank.

say abs(-1) abs/**/(-1) abs (-1) abs /**/(-1)
say (1)(2) (1) (2) (1)/**/(2)
a = .array~of(5, 6)
say a[2] a/**/[2] 'abc'~left(2) 'abc'~left/**/(2)
signal on syntax name e
interpret 'say a [2]'
e: say condition('o')~code
signal on syntax name f
interpret "say 'abc'~left (2)"
f: say condition('o')~code
